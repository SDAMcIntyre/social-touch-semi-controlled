## Spatial RF Data

### Vallbo et al. 1995 — Hairy skin Aβ mechanoreceptors

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `vallbo-et-al-1995-hairy-skin-ab-mechanoreceptors.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Vallbo, Olausson, Wessberg, Kakuda |
| **Journal** | The Journal of Physiology 483:783–95 |
| **Species / site** | Human, hairy forearm (lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve) and proximal hand dorsum |
| **Afferents** | SA-I (Merkel), SA-II (Ruffini), HFA, Field units, Pacinian |

Foundational characterisation of five myelinated mechanoreceptor types in
human hairy forearm skin using an automated scanning probe (1 mm diameter,
0.22–0.25 g, 0.23 mm track spacing) in 21 subjects.

**Key findings**

- SA-I (38% of sample): median RF area 11 mm², 2–4 well-separated high-sensitivity spots (individual spot median 0.8 mm², inter-spot distance 3.0 mm) corresponding to Merkel cell clusters
- SA-II (27%): small RFs (median 1.4 mm²), typically a single responsive spot; 8/10 units fired spontaneously
- Hair units (22%): median 113 mm², 10–33+ innervated hairs over large oval area, directional sensitivity (proximal probe movement more effective)
- Field units (9%): median 78 mm², numerous closely packed hotspots with diffuse borders
- Pacinian (4%): large, deep fields
- Forearm RA units (hair + field) have much larger RFs (78–113 mm²) than on hand dorsum (24 mm²) or face (17 mm²), while SA-I sizes are similar (~11 mm²) across regions

**Relevance** — Provides the classical punctate-probe RF baseline for the
exact body site used in the naturalistic study. The response-field expansion
is benchmarked against these values.

### Wessberg et al. 2003 — CT receptive field properties

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `wessberg-et-al-2003-ct-receptive-field-properties.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Wessberg, Olausson, Fernström, Vallbo |
| **Journal** | Journal of Neurophysiology 89:1567–1575 |
| **DOI** | 10.1152/jn.00256.2002 |
| **Species / site** | Human, hairy forearm skin |
| **Afferents** | CT |

Most detailed classical mapping of CT receptive field structure using
scanning probe (1 mm hemispherical tip) at 2 mm/s in 0.23 mm tracks at
controlled forces (2.2, 5, 20 mN), spike density on 0.5 mm grid. Nine
CT units recorded.

**Key findings**

- RFs composed of 1–9 discrete hotspots (mean 5), spanning 1–35 mm² (mean 14.7 ± 10.5 mm² at 5 mN)
- Number of spots correlates strongly with field size (r = 0.96), each spot adding ~4–5 mm²
- Field size force-dependent: doubling force from 2.2 to 5 mN nearly doubled area, but spatial structure (spot count/location) preserved
- Von Frey thresholds very low (0.3–2.5 mN); vigorous response to light stroking (45–84 imp/s)
- Twin maps from proximal/distal probe movements systematically shifted 0.4–1.4 mm (slow C-fiber conduction)
- Punctate, non-uniform structure suggests clusters of endings, not continuous mesh

**Relevance** — Classical CT RF baseline (mean 14.7 mm²) vs. naturalistic
response fields (632–662 mm²) gives the ~19–45× expansion factor for CTs.
Force-dependent field sizes directly relevant to interpreting naturalistic
contact-force variation.

### Johansson & Vallbo 1979 — Glabrous skin mechanoreceptors

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `johansson-vallbo-1979-glabrous-skin-mechanoreceptors.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Johansson, Vallbo |
| **Journal** | The Journal of Physiology 286:283–300 |
| **Species / site** | Human, glabrous hand skin (palm and fingers) |
| **Afferents** | RA (FA-I), PC (FA-II), SA-I, SA-II |

Primary data study: 334 single units via median nerve microneurography in
40 subjects. Mapped relative and absolute innervation densities across hand
regions.

**Key findings**

- RA most common (42.8%), then SA-I (25.1%), SA-II (19.2%), PC (12.9%)
- Pronounced proximo-distal density gradient: palm ~58 units/cm², fingertip ~241 units/cm²
- Gradient driven by RA and SA-I (small, well-defined RFs); PC and SA-II distributed evenly
- ~17,000 total mechanoreceptive units estimated per hand
- Spatial distribution of RA/SA-I matches psychophysical two-point discrimination

**Relevance** — Foundational density and classification framework for glabrous
skin; establishes the reference against which hairy-skin measurements (both
classical and naturalistic) are compared.

### Johansson & Vallbo 1983 — Tactile sensibility review

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `johansson-vallbo-1983-tactile-sensibility-review.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Johansson, Vallbo |
| **Journal** | Trends in Neurosciences 6:27–32 |
| **DOI** | 10.1016/0166-2236(83)90011-5 |
| **Species / site** | Human, glabrous hand skin |
| **Afferents** | FA-I (Meissner), FA-II (Pacinian), SA-I (Merkel), SA-II (Ruffini) |

Canonical review establishing the four-type classification, functional
roles, and density mapping. Highlights microneurography's unique ability
to probe single-unit physiology and subjective experience simultaneously.

**Key findings**

- 4-type classification: FA-I and SA-I have small, sharply bordered RFs (2–8 mm diameter, high fingertip density ~140 FA-I and ~70 SA-I /cm²); FA-II and SA-II have large, diffuse fields
- ~17,000 mechanoreceptive units per hand; ~56% fast-adapting, ~44% slowly adapting
- SA-I exquisitely edge-sensitive (stronger response to edge crossing than uniform indentation)
- SA-II uniquely sensitive to lateral skin stretch direction; 94% respond to finger joint movements → proprioceptive role
- Single FA-I impulse can reach perceptual awareness (psychophysical = neuronal threshold)

**Relevance** — General framing context; the classification framework and
functional-role assignments readers bring to the paper.

### Johansson & Vallbo 1980 — Glabrous population spatial properties

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `johansson-vallbo-1980-glabrous-population-spatial-properties.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Johansson, Vallbo |
| **Journal** | Brain Research 184:353–366 |
| **DOI** | 10.1016/0006-8993(80)90804-5 |
| **Species / site** | Human, glabrous hand (fingertip, finger, palm) via median nerve |
| **Afferents** | RA (n=118), SA-I (n=66), SA-II (n=39), PC (n=32) — 255 units total |

Population-level reconstruction of RA and SA-I RF spatial properties
on the glabrous hand. Von Frey hair mapping at 4–5× threshold in 52
experiments (40 subjects). Builds a theoretical sheath model of RF
tiling to predict population responses to point, disc, and bar stimuli.

**Key findings**

- RF size medians: RA 12.6 mm², SA-I 11.0 mm², SA-II 58.9 mm², PC 101.3 mm² — all distributions positively skewed
- RA and SA-I fields roughly round/oval, ~75% with long axis along the longitudinal hand direction; SA-II and PC fields large with obscure boundaries
- RA fields showed significant proximo-distal size gradient: fingertip median 9.4 mm², finger 12.6 mm², palm 28.1 mm² (P < 0.001); SA-I fields did not vary significantly across regions
- Mean overlap of RA + SA-I combined: ~20 fields at fingertip, ~9 at finger, ~6 at palm — directly proportional to unit density
- Point stimulus on fingertip indents ~34 RA + SA-I fields spread over ~8 mm; disc stimuli <2 mm diameter activate similar numbers
- Bar stimuli activate ~1 additional unit per 2.2 mm (fingertip), 3.7 mm (finger), 4.6 mm (palm)
- Two-point discrimination capacity of the model matches psychophysical thresholds — spatial resolution higher than individual RF size predicts, due to population overlap
- Concept of "central field" introduced: the area of maximal sensitivity, ~coincident with receptor terminal location, where sensitivity drops steeply beyond 0.5 mm from border

**Relevance** — Provides the population overlap and spatial resolution
model for glabrous skin that the current study implicitly extends to
hairy skin with naturalistic stimuli. The sheath model's prediction
that many RFs are co-activated by a single contact point directly
supports interpreting the large naturalistic response fields as
population-level recruitment, not single-unit expansion. The
proximo-distal RF size gradient parallels the forearm's expected
spatial variation.

### Bai et al. 2015 — Field-LTMR organisation

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `bai-et-al-2015-field-ltmr-organization.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Bai, Lehnert, Liu, Neubarth, Dickendesher, Nwe, Cassidy, Woodbury, Ginty |
| **Journal** | Cell 163(7):1783–1795 |
| **DOI** | 10.1016/j.cell.2015.11.060 |
| **Species / site** | Mouse, dorsal thigh hairy skin (circumferential endings also confirmed in cat, dog, macaque) |
| **Afferents** | Aβ Field-LTMR (primary), Aβ RA-LTMR and SA1-LTMR (comparison) |

Identifies Aβ Field-LTMRs using intersectional genetic labelling — a major
but previously elusive class with NFH+ circumferential endings around hair
follicles. ~4% of DRG neurons.

**Key findings**

- Individual Aβ Field-LTMRs form 20–180 circumferential endings innervating hair follicles over ~3.1 mm² — the most anatomically expansive LTMR identified
- Highly sensitive to gentle skin stroking but categorically insensitive to hair deflection and relatively insensitive to innocuous skin indentation
- RFs consist of large, spotty arrays of weakly mechanosensitive hotspots (half-width ~60 μm) separated by insensitive stretches
- FEM and TEM show circumferential endings reside in an outer collagen-sheathed region that dampens indentation while preserving stretch/stroking sensitivity
- Spike initiation sites located at variable, often large distances from terminals — unlike other Aβ LTMRs
- Prevalent across mammalian species (mice, cats, dogs, macaques)

**Relevance** — Establishes the morphological and physiological basis for
stroke-sensitive, large-RF mechanoreceptors in hairy skin. The spotty
hotspot organisation and stroking selectivity directly inform interpretation
of naturalistic response fields on human forearm skin.

### Kuehn et al. 2019 — LTMR tiling and somatotopic alignment

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `kuehn-et-al-2019-ltmr-tiling-somatotopic-alignment.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Kuehn, Meltzer, Abraira, Ho, Ginty |
| **Journal** | PNAS 116(19):9168–9177 |
| **DOI** | 10.1073/pnas.1901378116 |
| **Species / site** | Mouse, trunk hairy skin (back, abdomen, thigh) |
| **Afferents** | C-LTMR, Aδ-LTMR, Aβ Field-LTMR, Aβ SA1-LTMR, Aβ RA-LTMR |

Genetic sparse-labelling reveals peripheral tiling and central somatotopic
projection patterns for all five hairy-skin LTMR subtypes.

**Key findings**

- Four of five subtypes (C-LTMR, Aδ-LTMR, Aβ Field-LTMR, Aβ SA1-LTMR) are largely tiled (non-overlapping) with respect to hair follicles; Aβ RA-LTMRs are the exception with redundant multi-neuron innervation
- Isoneuronal branches show self-avoidance (rarely innervate the same follicle), except C-LTMRs which show high isoneuronal overlap
- Central projections show dramatic rostrocaudal elongation vs. peripheral layout (15:1 for C-LTMRs, up to 40:1 for Aβ subtypes)
- Central projections of LTMRs innervating the same skin region exhibit striking somatotopic adjacency in the dorsal horn despite random DRG cell body placement
- Guard hairs receive more dual/triple innervation from Aβ RA-LTMRs and Field-LTMRs than other follicle types

**Relevance** — The tiling principle may explain spatial consistency of
per-gesture response fields across repeated touches. The LTMR-recipient zone
of the dorsal horn is proposed as a major sensory processing centre analogous
to the retina.

### Ebbesen & Froemke 2022 — 3D videography social touch (mice)

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `ebbesen-froemke-2022-3d-videography-social-touch.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Ebbesen, Froemke |
| **Journal** | Nature Communications 13:593 |
| **DOI** | 10.1038/s41467-022-28153-7 |
| **Species / site** | Mouse, barrel cortex (silicon probes); full body surface tracked |
| **Afferents** | N/A — cortical single units, not peripheral afferents |

"3DDD Social Mouse Tracker": multi-view depth cameras + deep learning +
GPU-accelerated particle-filter optimisation for 3D posture tracking of
multiple unmarked mice at ~2 mm precision, 60 fps, in darkness (IR).
Coupled with barrel cortex electrophysiology.

**Key findings**

- 52% of barrel cortex neurons encoded at least one behavioural feature (median 5 features per neuron), including social touch distance, partner orientation, self-posture, and movement
- Poisson generative model reveals simultaneous tuning to social touch, orientation, and posture features of both animals
- 99.8% tracking accuracy; robust identity maintenance during close interactions (mounting, allo-grooming)
- Cleaning PSTHs by excluding temporally overlapping touch events dramatically increases detected modulation — naive PSTH approaches substantially underestimate social touch responses
- Population co-encoding: nose-to-nose, anogenital touch, and orientation angle form a tightly co-encoded cluster

**Relevance** — Closest methodological precursor: both combine depth-camera
3D tracking with neural recordings during naturalistic social touch. However,
Ebbesen & Froemke operate at cortical level in mice with keypoint-based
proximity (no mesh), while the Kinect + SLIM UV approach provides mesh-based
contact characterisation with peripheral afferent recordings in humans.

## Stimulus–Response Tuning

### Löken et al. 2009 — CT velocity–pleasantness curve

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `loken-et-al-2009-ct-velocity-pleasantness.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Löken, Wessberg, Morrison, McGlone, Olausson |
| **Journal** | Nature Neuroscience 12:547–8 |
| **DOI** | 10.1038/nn.2312 |
| **Species / site** | Human, hairy forearm and glabrous palm |
| **Afferents** | CT (n=20), SAI (15), SAII (12), hair (7), field (2) |

Landmark paper: 56 afferents recorded via microneurography with robotic
brush stimulation at controlled velocities (two forces: 0.2 and 0.4 N,
~43 stimuli per unit). Separate psychophysical pleasantness sessions (n=10).

**Key findings**

- CT: inverted-U velocity response peaking at ~1–3 cm/s (quadratic fit peak 2.8 cm/s); negative quadratic fit in 14/16 units (R² = 0.44, range 0.29–0.60)
- Myelinated afferents (SAI, SAII, hair, field): monotonic increase with velocity, no pleasantness correlation
- CT firing frequency explains 70% of pleasantness variance (Pearson R² = 0.70, P = 0.00063)
- Pleasantness significantly lower on palm (few CTs) vs. forearm, especially at 3 cm/s
- Inverted-U absent on palm, confirming CT involvement

**Relevance** — Velocity tuning reference for the study's velocity-grid
analysis. The inverted-U at ~3 cm/s vs. monotonic Aβ response provides the
benchmark for subtype discrimination in the naturalistic velocity range.

### Ackerley et al. 2014 — CT velocity × temperature tuning

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `ackerley-et-al-2014-ct-velocity-temperature.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Ackerley, Backlund Wasling, Liljencrantz, Olausson, Johnson, Wessberg |
| **Journal** | The Journal of Neuroscience 34(8):2879–2883 |
| **DOI** | 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2847-13.2014 |
| **Species / site** | Human, hairy forearm (left antebrachial cutaneous nerve) |
| **Afferents** | CT (n=20), myelinated hair/field/SA1/SA2 (n=8) |

Bimodal (mechano-thermal) stimulation of CT afferents using a rotary
probe with Peltier-controlled temperature (18°C, 32°C, 42°C) at five
velocities (0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30 cm/s), 0.4 N force, ~5 cm² contact.
Separate psychophysical pleasantness sessions (n=30).

**Key findings**

- CT firing peaks at 3 cm/s AND 32°C (neutral skin temperature); the inverted-U velocity curve is present at all temperatures but strongest at neutral
- At neutral temperature: significant negative quadratic regression (R² = 0.10, F₍₂,₁₉₃₎ = 10.18, p < 0.001); similar fits at cool (R² = 0.08) and warm (R² = 0.10)
- Significant main effects of both velocity (F₍₄,₁₃₂₀₎ = 34.75, p < 0.001) and temperature (F₍₂,₁₃₂₀₎ = 110.30, p < 0.001), plus a significant interaction (F₍₈,₁₃₂₀₎ = 5.66, p < 0.001)
- CT firing–pleasantness correlation significant only at neutral temperature (p = 0.003, R² = 0.96); no correlation at cool or warm
- Myelinated hair afferents: monotonic velocity increase, no temperature modulation — positive quadratic fits (R² = 0.85–0.92)
- Mean CT conduction velocity 0.76 m/s (±0.06 SEM; range 0.30–1.19 m/s)

**Relevance** — Extends Löken 2009 by adding temperature as a second
stimulus dimension. The neutral-temperature optimum supports interpreting
the current study's naturalistic skin-to-skin contact (delivered at ambient
skin temperature ~32°C) as near-optimal for CT activation. The CT vs.
myelinated dissociation (quadratic vs. monotonic, temperature-sensitive vs.
insensitive) reinforces subtype discrimination criteria.

### Vallbo et al. 1999 — CT as second tactile system

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `vallbo-et-al-1999-ct-second-tactile-system.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Vallbo, Olausson, Wessberg |
| **Journal** | Journal of Neurophysiology 81:2753–63 |
| **Species / site** | Human, hairy forearm (radial aspect, lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve) |
| **Afferents** | CT (n=27 low-threshold), high-threshold C nociceptors (n=11) |

First comprehensive characterisation of CT afferents as a distinct tactile
system. Sample of 38 C afferents split by bimodal von Frey threshold
distribution. Recorded in 17 subjects.

**Key findings**

- CT thresholds 0.3–2.5 mN (median 1.3 mN), clearly separable from C nociceptors (~40 mN) by bimodal distribution
- CT responds vigorously to slow-moving innocuous stimuli (50–100 imp/s brush stroking) but poorly to fast stimuli — opposite of myelinated Aβ
- CT does not discriminate blunt-probe vs. pin-prick (responds equally to both); C nociceptors respond preferentially to pin-prick
- Novel delayed acceleration response: after initial adaptation, CT resumes irregular firing peaking ~40 imp/s over ~40 s
- Prominent fatigue with repeated stimulation
- CT constituted 71% of C afferent sample → not an evolutionary vestige; authors propose affiliative/limbic functional role

**Relevance** — Foundational paper defining the CT system; the small classical
RFs (1–35 mm²) contrast with the study's naturalistic response fields
(632–662 mm²), and the velocity/force preferences inform why the study's
stroking parameters effectively activate CTs.

### Xu et al. 2025 — Aβ gesture discrimination

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `xu-et-al-2025-ab-gesture-discrimination.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Xu*, Hauser*, Nagi*, Jablonski, Rezaei, Jarocka, Marshall, Olausson, McIntyre, Gerling |
| **Journal** | bioRxiv (preprint) |
| **DOI** | 10.1101/2023.07.22.549516 |
| **Species / site** | Human, hairy forearm and hand (radial nerve above elbow) |
| **Afferents** | SA-II (n=4), HFA (n=7), Field (n=5), CT (n=6), FA-II (n=5), MS (n=12) — 39 units, 819 trials |

Six social touch expressions (attention, happiness, calming, love, sadness,
gratitude) delivered by trained experimenters; classified with 1D-CNN on
binary spike trains and SVM on five extracted firing features.

**Key findings**

- SA-II and HFA achieve 70–80% classification accuracy for six social expressions (CNN and SVM), matching human perceptual performance
- CT, FA-II, and muscle spindle afferents perform near chance
- Only 3–4 s of firing (out of 10 s) provides sufficient discriminative information — 1–2 orders of magnitude longer timescale than non-social touch discrimination
- SA-II and HFA tolerate spike-timing jitter up to 10 ms; 50 ms transforms envelope → misclassification
- Most informative segments include both contact periods and prolonged non-contact gaps (gesture rhythm/temporal structure)

**Relevance** — Most directly comparable study: same body site, same touch
paradigm, overlapping afferent types. Gerling reports temporal discrimination;
the current study adds spatial response field mapping with UV parameterisation
and automated boundary extraction. Complementary, not competing.

### Hauser & Gerling 2019 — Human touch tracking + nerve responses

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `hauser-gerling-2019-human-touch-nerve-responses.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Hauser, Nagi, McIntyre, Israr, Olausson, Gerling |
| **Journal** | IEEE World Haptics Conference (WHC) 2019 |
| **Species / site** | Human, hairy forearm (radial nerve) |
| **Afferents** | CT, PC (Pacinian) — case study of one unit each |

Conference paper combining 2D video camera + 3D Leap Motion IR tracking to
quantify four physical touch attributes (contact, depth, contact area, shear
velocity) during four gesture types (single-finger tap, multi-finger tap,
multi-finger stroke, whole-hand hold), synchronised with microneurography.

**Key findings**

- CT afferent fired primarily during multi-finger stroking at high shear velocity (peak 43.8 Hz), rarely during tapping
- PC afferent fired synchronously with each tap and at stroke onset over RF (peak 362.3 Hz)
- Non-contact optical tracking avoids placing barriers on skin that alter natural touch
- Algorithmic correction of Leap Motion data using 2D video improved occluded-finger tracking
- Physical touch attributes covary during naturalistic gestures (area and depth intimately related) → multivariate analysis needed
- First reported method for tracking natural human-to-human gestures with simultaneous microneurography

**Relevance** — Direct methodological precursor. Leap Motion captures joint
positions but lacks mesh-based contact area and depth information that the
Kinect + SLIM UV approach provides. The covariance of touch attributes
motivates the multivariate feature extraction in the current study.

## Contact Area and Texture

### Gescheider et al. 2005 — Contact area and threshold

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `gescheider-et-al-2005-contact-area-threshold.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Gescheider, Guclu, Sexton, Karalunas, Fontana |
| **Journal** | Somatosensory and Motor Research |
| **DOI** | 10.1080/08990220500420236 |
| **Species / site** | Human, glabrous hand (thenar eminence) |
| **Afferents** | Pacinian (P channel, 300 Hz) |

Three psychophysical experiments in four subjects using 300 Hz vibration
with contactors 0.05–1.5 cm², two-alternative forced-choice tracking.

**Key findings**

- ~13 dB total spatial summation: ~8 dB neural integration + ~5 dB probability summation
- Probability summation over space ~4 dB, over time ~1 dB; neural integration the largest single factor
- ~3 dB threshold decrease per doubling of contactor size
- Threshold variability 2× larger for small contactor (3.85 dB vs. 1.83 dB SD)
- Psychometric function slope increases with contactor size (consistent with probability summation reducing trial-to-trial variability)
- Even controlling for probability summation, large contactor still yields lower thresholds → direct evidence for central neural integration

**Relevance** — Establishes that contact area critically determines tactile
thresholds through both receptor recruitment and neural integration,
supporting the interpretation that whole-hand contact activates
mechanoreceptors far more effectively than punctate probes.

### Saal et al. 2017 — TouchSim whole-hand model

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `saal-et-al-2017-touchsim-whole-hand.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Saal, Delhaye, Rayhaun, Bensmaia |
| **Journal** | PNAS 114(28):E5693–E5702 |
| **DOI** | 10.1073/pnas.1704856114 |
| **Species / site** | Monkey (rhesus macaque) data for fitting; model applied to human glabrous hand |
| **Afferents** | SA1, RA, PC — SA2 explicitly excluded |

TouchSim: computational model combining quasistatic elastic skin mechanics
+ surface wave propagation with leaky integrate-and-fire spiking models
(13 free parameters per afferent, fit to macaque electrophysiology).
~12,500 afferents tiled across hand at known densities.

**Key findings**

- Simulated responses closely match biology: firing rates R² ~0.91, temporal precision 2–8 ms
- ~12,500 afferents on palmar hand; PC:RA:SA1 ratio ≈ 4:2:1 per fingertip (~1,000 total)
- Dynamic stimuli activate PC across the entire hand; SA1 and RA respond only locally
- Combining SA1 and RA populations improves edge orientation decoding vs. either alone (latency ~40–50 ms matches human data)
- Emergent properties: surround suppression, edge enhancement, adaptation (not explicitly built in)
- Key limitation: glabrous skin only, no SA2, derived from macaque data

**Relevance** — The force-in-rates / texture-in-timing framework and
population-level spatial recruitment patterns are relevant for interpreting
the study's feature extraction. The model's skin mechanics approach offers a
template for building analogous hairy-skin simulations on UV-parameterised
forearm surfaces.

## Reviews and Context

### Marshall & McGlone 2020 — CT spinal pathway review

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `marshall-mcglone-2020-ct-spinal-pathway-review.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Marshall, McGlone |
| **Journal** | Neuroscience Insights vol 15 |
| **DOI** | 10.1177/2633105520925072 |
| **Species / site** | Human (cordotomy patients, HSAN patients) + rodent dorsal horn references |
| **Afferents** | CT, Aβ LTMR |

Commentary/review synthesising the authors' anterolateral cordotomy study
with hereditary sensory neuropathy and rodent dorsal horn data. Challenges
the conventional view that CT signals ascend via the lamina I-spinothalamic
tract.

**Key findings**

- Anterolateral cordotomy dramatically impairs pain, temperature, itch (L1-STT modalities) but leaves the inverted-U velocity-pleasantness curve completely intact
- CT signals may ascend via an alternative pathway (e.g. post-synaptic dorsal column, PSDC)
- Up to 25% of fibres in the mammalian dorsal column pathway are unmyelinated — plausible substrate for CT relay outside L1-STT
- HSAN-V patients (congenitally lacking unmyelinated afferents) do not show the inverted-U → CT input necessary for developmental anchoring
- CT may primarily serve homeostatic signalling (safety/well-being via autonomic pathways) rather than directly encoding pleasantness
- Nurturing touch via CT stimulation in early life shapes the social brain

**Relevance** — Redefines the central processing framework for CT afferents:
if CT signals do not use the spinothalamic tract, models of CT spatial coding
and its relation to affective touch must be re-examined. Frames why per-gesture
response field shapes may differ between CT and Aβ afferent classes.

### Morrison 2016 — CT affective touch: brain networks

| | |
|---|---|
| **File** | `morrison-2016-ct-affective-touch-brain-networks.pdf` |
| **Authors** | Morrison (book chapter in Olausson et al. eds, *Affective Touch and the Neurophysiology of CT Afferents*) |
| **Journal** | Springer |
| **Species / site** | Human, hairy skin (primarily forearm) |
| **Afferents** | CT, with Aβ reference |

Review of brain networks for CT-mediated affective touch, integrating
neuroimaging (fMRI), psychophysics, and clinical evidence.

**Key findings**

- CT-optimal velocities (1–10 cm/s) produce strongest posterior insula activation
- Affective touch network: posterior insula, medial prefrontal cortex, superior temporal sulcus
- CT-mediated touch involved in social bonding, attachment, emotional regulation
- Individual differences correlate with autistic traits and interoceptive sensitivity

**Relevance** — Central neural context for CT processing; frames the social-
touch paradigm's ecological validity and why CT response field spatial
organisation may serve different functional goals than Aβ fields.

**Key findings**

- LTMRs fall into subtypes classified by conduction velocity (Aβ, Aδ, C), adaptation rate, and end-organ association
- Each hair follicle type (guard, awl/auchene, zigzag) receives a unique, invariant combination of LTMR subtype innervation → discrete mechanosensory units
- C-LTMRs found exclusively in hairy skin, maximally activated by slow gentle stroking, signal via insular cortex (not S1)
- Different stimuli (poke, stroke, breeze, caress) generate distinct ensemble activity codes across subtypes; slow caress creates unique "LTMR caress ensemble"
- LTMR inputs converge in somatotopically organised columnar processing units in dorsal horn
- Spinal cord proposed as early integration site: LTMR ensembles processed before ascending to brain via distinct pathways (direct dorsal column, PSDC, SCT)

classical taxonomy and that different stimuli produce unique LTMR ensemble

differences across afferent types.
